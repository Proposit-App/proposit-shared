Each assessment is more than a label. Beside it I read a short plain-language
explanation of what that assessment says — and, just as importantly, what it does
not say.

The two axes are explained separately, in their own terms. The conclusion
explanation is about the proposition: what it means for the conclusion to come
out true, false or unknown under the values I supplied, and why unknown is a
statement about how much I settled rather than a mark against the argument. The
argument explanation is about whether the author established it: what it means
for an argument to reach its conclusion, to fail to reach it, or to have premises
that cannot all hold at once — and, when it doesn't reach it, what the particular
reason means.

The explanations are written for someone who has not studied logic. They name the
idea a reader can look up — validity, soundness, a conditional satisfied by a
false antecedent, a counterexample — and say it in ordinary words first.

Nothing here re-collapses the two axes into a single grade, and nothing here
allocates fault. An explanation describes what the assessment reports; it does
not tell me the argument is good or bad, or that I got something wrong.

An explanation states only what the assessment established, and never names a
cause I could act on when there is none. Where two readings share one assessment
and only one of them leaves me something to do, the explanation I read is the one
that fits my own review: if I have answered everything the argument asks, it says
so, rather than sending me back to settle claims I have already settled.
