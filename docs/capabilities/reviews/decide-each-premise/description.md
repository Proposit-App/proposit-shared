After deciding the claims, I walk through the argument's premises and accept or
reject each, optionally with a reason. Each premise gets exactly one decision —
I judge the premise as a whole, aimed at its outermost decidable operator, not
each operator nested inside it.

Accepting a premise grants that step of the reasoning. Rejecting it strikes the
premise: it leaves the set the argument is assessed on while staying in the
record, still shown to me and to anyone reading my review. A rejection asserts
nothing — not that the premise is false, and nothing about either side of it. It
is how I say the step doesn't carry here, for example that it generally holds
but not in this case.

A premise that states a constraint rather than a step of reasoning is granted or
declined instead; declining withholds the constraint rather than denying it.
